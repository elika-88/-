How Evidence Becomes Knowledge: An Introduction to Research Methods

1. Questions, observations, and hypotheses

Research begins with a question that can be answered using evidence. A useful question identifies what we want to understand and makes clear what observations would matter. Consider a teacher who notices that students using a short practice quiz appear to remember more vocabulary the following week. This observation is a starting point. It is not yet evidence that the practice quiz caused better memory. The students may differ in motivation, prior knowledge, time spent studying, or other ways. The research question could be: does completing a short practice quiz improve vocabulary recall after one week compared with spending the same time rereading?

A hypothesis is a proposed answer that can be tested. In this example, the hypothesis predicts higher recall after practice quizzes. A useful prediction also describes an outcome that would count against the hypothesis. If otherwise comparable groups have similar recall, or if the rereading group performs better, the evidence would not support the prediction as stated. Researchers should write down the question, prediction, and measurement plan before seeing the results. This helps distinguish a planned test from an explanation created after the outcome is known. An unexpected result can be valuable, but it should be described as unexpected.

2. Variables and operational definitions

A variable is a characteristic that can take different values. The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed in an experiment. In the vocabulary example, it is the study activity: practice quiz or rereading. The dependent variable is the measured outcome, such as the number of words recalled correctly after one week. Controlled variables are conditions kept the same where possible. Both groups might study the same words, use the same study time, and complete the same final test under similar conditions.

An operational definition specifies how an idea will be measured or implemented. Saying that an activity improves learning is too vague for a particular experiment. The team must define learning for this test: for example, correctly writing the meanings of twenty words without looking at a list. This definition does not capture every possible aspect of learning. It creates an observable outcome that another person can understand and repeat. The researchers should report the definition and avoid claiming that their measure covers abilities they did not assess. Recognizing a word on a multiple-choice test and producing its meaning from memory are different tasks. Results from one should not automatically be described as results from the other.

3. Comparison groups and random assignment

A comparison group provides a reference condition. Without one, improvement after an activity may have several explanations. Students might improve simply because they encountered the vocabulary again, because the second test was easier, or because they studied outside class. Comparing practice quizzes with rereading for the same duration makes the question more specific. The comparison is between these two activities under the study conditions, not between studying and every possible alternative.

Random assignment places participants into groups through a chance process. Its purpose is to reduce systematic differences between the groups before the activity starts. It does not guarantee that two small groups will have identical characteristics. Researchers should still describe important starting characteristics and report practical problems such as participants dropping out. Random assignment and random sampling serve different purposes. Assignment concerns which study condition a participant receives. Sampling concerns how participants are selected from a larger population. A study may randomly assign students from one class while still having a sample that does not represent students in other schools. Clear reporting keeps these two ideas separate.

4. Measurement reliability and validity

Measurement reliability concerns consistency. If an instrument is used under the same conditions, does it produce similar readings? Measurement validity concerns whether the interpretation of a measure matches the concept the researchers intend to study. These are related but different questions. A scale that consistently adds two kilograms illustrates why consistent readings alone do not establish accuracy. Calibration against a known reference can reveal this kind of systematic error.

In the vocabulary study, the researchers need a scoring rule. If one scorer accepts a close synonym while another rejects it, the outcome may depend on who marks the answer. A shared rubric, examples of acceptable responses, and checks of agreement can improve consistency. The researchers also need to ask whether the test measures the intended knowledge. A test dominated by difficult instructions may partly measure reading comprehension instead of vocabulary recall. Improving a measurement procedure can be more useful than collecting additional data with a flawed instrument. A larger sample cannot automatically remove systematic measurement bias.

5. Variation, samples, and uncertainty

People vary, and measurements also vary. Two students who use the same study method may recall different numbers of words. Even the same student may perform differently on different days. A sample provides a limited set of observations rather than a complete picture of every possible participant and situation. A new sample may therefore produce a different numerical result even when the procedure is similar.

Larger samples can reduce the influence of some forms of random variation, but sample size is not a substitute for a good design. Recruiting more participants from the same narrow group does not by itself make the group representative. Collecting more measurements with the same biased instrument does not correct the bias. Researchers should describe both the size of a sample and the way it was obtained. They should also describe missing data. If students with low initial scores are more likely to leave one condition, comparing only the students who remain may give a misleading impression. Uncertainty should be reported as part of the result rather than treated as an embarrassment to hide.

6. Association, causation, and alternative explanations

An association means that variables vary together in the observed data. Students who complete more practice questions may also receive higher scores. That association alone does not establish that additional practice questions caused the higher scores. More motivated students might both practice more and prepare in other ways. Prior knowledge might influence both the decision to practice and the final score. These possibilities are alternative explanations that the study needs to consider.

A well-designed experiment can strengthen a causal interpretation by deliberately changing an activity and reducing competing explanations. Its conclusions are still tied to the actual design, participants, measurements, and conditions. Researchers should distinguish the observed result from the broader interpretation they propose. A statement such as the practice group recalled more words on this test is narrower than a claim that practice quizzes always improve all forms of learning. The first may be directly supported by the study; the second extends beyond what this example measures. Careful language helps readers see where the evidence ends and a new hypothesis begins.

7. Ethical treatment and privacy

Ethical research considers how the study affects participants. Informed consent means that participants receive understandable information about the procedure and relevant risks before deciding whether to take part. Participation should be voluntary. Participants should know how to ask questions and how to withdraw. In an educational setting, the relationship between a teacher and students can create pressure, so researchers need to explain how a decision about participation relates to ordinary class activities and assessment.

Privacy also requires practical choices about data. Researchers should collect information that is needed for the research question and limit access to identifiable records. A participant code can help separate a name from a score sheet, but a code does not automatically make all data anonymous. If a separate list connects the code to a name, that list still needs protection. Reports should avoid identifying individuals through descriptions or small combinations of characteristics. The team should decide how records will be stored, who can access them, and how long they are needed. These decisions belong in the study plan, not only in a response after a problem occurs.

8. Transparent reporting and replication

A research report should explain the question, participants, procedure, measurement, results, and limitations. Readers need enough detail to understand what was actually done. Reporting only the most favorable outcome can hide important context. If the team measured immediate recall and one-week recall, it should clearly distinguish them. If an analysis was changed after examining the data, that change should be described. Transparent reporting lets other people evaluate how strongly the observations support the conclusion.

Replication means repeating a study or an important part of it to examine whether the finding is consistent. A repetition may closely follow the original procedure, or it may deliberately test the idea in a different setting. These approaches answer related but different questions. A result that changes between studies calls for investigation rather than an automatic accusation that one team acted improperly. Differences in participants, instructions, measurement, or chance variation may matter.

The central habit in this lecture is to connect each claim to the evidence that supports it. Ask what was measured, what was compared, what alternative explanations remain, and which limitations apply. Good research does not require pretending that uncertainty has disappeared. It requires making the reasoning visible, treating participants responsibly, and stating conclusions at a level that the evidence can justify.
